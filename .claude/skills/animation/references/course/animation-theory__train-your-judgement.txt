Train your judgementAI can write animation code. What it can’t do is see. It doesn’t know that ease-in shouldn’t be used for entry animations, or that 500ms enter transition on a dialog is probably too slow.
It produces motion that works, but feels mediocre, and if you can’t tell the difference, you’ll ship it. You’ll settle for good enough, and that’s not good enough.
This lesson trains your ability to spot what’s wrong, name it, and fix it. Each exercise shows two animations side by side. Your job is to pick the better one, write down why you chose it, then see my breakdown.
Choosing A or B is easy, but putting into words why one is better trains your ability to articulate your judgement, and that will be incredibly valuable in the future. So take your time and take this lesson seriously. It will pay off.

What to look for in easing

Entries should decelerate (ease-out), not accelerate (ease-in).
Built-in CSS curves are almost never strong enough. Use custom curves.
Asymmetric curves feel more alive than symmetric ones.
When an animation feels flat, the curve is probably too weak.

What to look for in duration

Too fast is as bad as too slow. There’s a trackability threshold.
Frequency of use matters. Something used 50x a day needs speed, not delight.
Exits should be shorter and simpler than entries.
Duration scales with element size and distance traveled.

What to look for in hover & tap

hover:scale-105 is almost always too much. 1-2% is enough.
300ms is too slow for hovers. Use 100-150ms.
translateY on hover should be on a child element, not the target itself.
Buttons usually benefit the most when they have both hover and press feedback.
Press scale: 0.97, not 0.9.

What to look for in spatial awareness

Exit direction should match entry direction.
Transform-origin should be on the trigger, not center.
Navigation direction should match the mental model.
Don’t start from scale(0). Use 0.85-0.95.
